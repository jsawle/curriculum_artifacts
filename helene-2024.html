    Why the Appalachians?

    Hurricane Helene, September 2024. Why did the worst floods and landslides happen in the mountains of North Carolina, nearly 400 miles from where Helene came ashore?

        For teachers

        What the 3D view shows
        • Rain so far. The blue shading on the ground is the rain that had fallen since 8 am EDT on Tuesday 24 September, and it grows as the timeline plays. It is made from NOAA's hourly radar estimates (MRMS) at 312 towns and river gauges, prepared for NOAA's Helene in Southern Appalachia dashboard, spread between those places by inverse-distance weighting. It covers the southern Appalachians only. In step 5 it builds up again from Wednesday night, alongside the rivers, and step 6 keeps the same moment, so the map does not change between them. Click the map to read the value at a spot.
        • Steep ground. Slope worked out from Esri World Elevation on a grid about 1.2 km apart, so it describes the steepness of the landscape (mountain sides, the Blue Ridge escarpment), not of single hillsides. Each grid cell is drawn as a solid block with a thin bright amber edge and see-through amber inside, so the rain beneath still shows.
        • Heavy rain on steep ground. Not drawn on the map (it made the map too busy). The Test it tab and the "It all lined up" card count the landslides in places where the total rain and the steepness are both above the thresholds set in Test it, and compare that with the area those places cover. Students can change the thresholds.
        • Spatial correlation panel (step 7). A small panel at the top left of the map lists the four factors (soaked ground, Helene's track, the Blue Ridge, steep slopes) and marks each as the story reaches it; at the end it adds the Test it result and, for the conclusion, a one-line summary of the climate evidence. It says the same as the card, and folds away with its title.
        • River gauges. Each gauge is a column whose height follows the river's stage, measured every 15 minutes by the USGS (the USGS's checked, approved values). Each column is scaled to that river's highest stage before Helene, so the white disc (the old record) is at the same height for every gauge and columns can be compared: a column halfway to its disc is at half its record stage. Stage is measured from a fixed point at each gauge, not from the river bed, so this compares each river with its own past, not depths between rivers. Colour shows the NWS flood category: blue below flood stage, then yellow minor, orange moderate and red major flooding. A grey column means the gauge stopped reporting. The River chart gives the actual heights in feet. In the step 5 close-up, the French Broad at Asheville and the Swannanoa at Biltmore gauges are labelled with their water level, one label names the white discs (both records date from the 1916 flood), and Asheville is named; the other place and gauge names are hidden in this close-up, to keep it readable.
        • Rivers. The main rivers come from NHDPlus, the national map of streams, whose lines are drawn in the direction the water flows. Each river is drawn wider when the nearest USGS gauge on it measures more water; between gauges the flow is shared out in proportion to NHDPlus's estimate of each stretch's average flow, so widths between gauges are estimates. The white arrows move downstream and speed up as the river rises above its usual flow; they show direction, not the real speed of the water. Where no USGS gauge on the same river fits (for example the North Toe through Spruce Pine, the Cane, the Green, and the upper Broad River, the "Rocky Broad", through Chimney Rock), the river is a grey dashed line with steady grey arrows: it shows which way the water went, not how much. The Flow chart in step 5 shows the measured flow at each gauge in cubic feet per second, with the highest flow recorded there before Helene from the USGS peak-flow file.
        • Landslides. Points from the USGS preliminary landslide inventory. It does not record when each slide happened, so the page shows them from the morning of 27 September, when most occurred.
        • Weather pattern. High pressure (the Bermuda High) and low pressure in the upper air are drawn in approximate positions from the NHC report ("a subtropical ridge centered over the western Atlantic"; "a deep-layer cut-off low centered over the Tennessee Valley"). The low was still forming on 24–25 September (NHC discussion 8: "a mid-latitude trough/developing cut-off low over the Mississippi valley") but is drawn from the start to keep the picture simple. The stalled front (NHC: "from the central Gulf Coast northward towards the Tennessee Valley and southern Appalachians from 25–27 September") is mentioned in step 1 but not drawn, to keep the map simple. None of these are traced from weather maps.
        • Moisture stream. The light-blue streaks sketch the narrow stream of tropical moisture that NOAA's CW3E centre describes as "AR-like poleward transport of enhanced water vapor on the east side of Helene", carried "into the Southern Appalachians on 26 and 27 September", and that Climate.gov describes as squeezed between Helene's anticlockwise spin and the Bermuda High's clockwise spin. The page draws it as a curved band from Helene's east side to the Blue Ridge from 25 September until Helene's own spin takes over; its exact width and path are a sketch. Scientists call this kind of stream an atmospheric river; "conveyor belt" here is a plain-language image, not the meteorological term "warm conveyor belt".
        • Storm and winds. The storm centre follows the NHC best track. The cloud spiral and the moving white streaks are drawings of the storm's anticlockwise spin, not measured winds. The streaks ride about 1.5 km above the ground, so they rise over the mountains, and they turn blue only where the radar measured at least 0.25 in of rain in that hour, so where the "conveyor belt" drops its rain comes from real data. In step 4 the page adds extra streaks over the Blue Ridge so the lift and the rain are easier to see close up; this does not mean the wind was stronger there.
        • Terrain. Mountains are drawn 2× taller by default. Use the switch at the top of the map (it appears when zoomed in) to see true height.
        Limitations
        • The rain map is spread between 312 radar points (185 of them in the mountains shown), mostly towns and river gauges in valleys, so it smooths out small, very wet spots and the wettest ridges. Against rain gauges it is close in some places and low in others: Asheville airport measured 13.98 in (the map shows about 14.6 in there), but Spruce Pine measured 24.1 in (map 15.6 in), Mount Mitchell 24.4 in (map 18.9 in) and Busick 30.78 in (map about 20 in). So the map is lowest where the rain was heaviest.
        • The landslide inventory is preliminary and misses slides hidden by forest or too small to see from satellites. Some points are at the damage site, not where the slide began.
        • Step 7 compares areas, so it shows that landslides and heavy rain on steep ground happened in the same places. It cannot, by itself, prove that one caused the other; the science of why is in step 4 (orographic rain) and step 6 (landslides).
        • Step 7 slows the clock over the four hours before 9 am on Friday 27 September and stops it there, to give time to look at Helene beside the Blue Ridge. Helene itself did not slow down: from the NHC track, its centre was moving at more than 30 mph. The step ends at 8 pm that day; less than 0.2 in more rain fell anywhere in the study area afterwards, so the map is the storm total.
        • River heights from different sources differ slightly: the USGS provisional peak at Asheville was 24.67 ft; the approved value used here is 24.82 ft, matching the NHC report's 24.8 ft. At many gauges the flood was too high or dangerous to measure flow directly, so the USGS worked out the peak flows afterwards (for example from high-water marks); the chart dashes the values the USGS marks as estimated. The Nolichucky gauge at Embreeville stopped recording flow at 11:15 am on 27 September and is still provisional.
        • NOAA's National Water Model was checked as a source of river flow and not used: it matched gauges that kept reporting, but gave only a quarter to just over half of the measured peak at several of the worst-hit mountain gauges, including Asheville and the Swannanoa.
        • The rainfall, river and gauge data are read from saved copies in the site (three files, about 120 KB to download), made from the live NOAA, USGS and NHDPlus services by automatic scripts; if the NOAA copy is missing, the page reads the live NOAA services instead. The landslides, elevation and maps are live services. River data load only at steps 5 and 7, and the mountains are measured from step 3, to keep the start quick on school networks. Some gauges stopped reporting during the flood; the USGS approved records used here fill most of those gaps (for example, the French Broad at Blantyre now shows its peak of 28.4 ft, above its 1916 record).
        Climate change and Helene (step 7's conclusion)

        The conclusion answers a question students often ask: was this a climate change event? It keeps two questions apart. The four factors in steps 1 to 7 explain where the damage was worst; climate studies ask whether the rain was heavier than it would have been without human-caused warming. Attribution studies compare the chances or size of an event in today's climate with a modelled climate without that warming; they cannot say whether a particular storm would have happened.

        • World Weather Attribution (rapid study, 9 October 2024, released before peer review): in both its regions, a coastal one and a seven-state inland one that includes the southern Appalachians, rain about 10% heavier because of climate change; inland three-day totals about 70% more likely; an inland rain event like Helene's now expected about once every 70 years (range 20 to 3,000); sea temperatures over the track made about 200 to 500 times more likely; maximum winds of similar storms about 11% (6.1 m/s) stronger.
        • Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (preliminary analysis, not peer reviewed, reported 1 to 3 October 2024): up to 50% more rain in parts of Georgia and the Carolinas, and rain like that observed up to 20 times more likely there.
        • Debate. David Easterling (NOAA) said WWA averaged rain over too large an area, and: "It's very scientifically sound to say the rainfall totals were greater due to climate change. Whether it's 10% or 50% is debatable — that's hard to determine precisely" (WFAE, December 2024). Different areas, methods and stages of review are one reason the numbers differ, which makes a good discussion for students.
        • IPCC (AR6, 2021, an assessment of published research): "human-induced climate change increases heavy precipitation associated with tropical cyclones (high confidence), but data limitations inhibit clear detection of past trends on the global scale"; there is "low confidence in long-term (multi-decadal to centennial) trends in the frequency of all-category tropical cyclones"; extreme daily rain is projected to intensify "by about 7% for each 1°C of global warming".

        The card's wording ("probably made Helene's rain heavier, by an amount that is still uncertain") follows that evidence: the direction is well supported, the size is not settled, and none of it explains why the worst damage was in these mountains.

        About and versions
        Accessibility
        • The page is being built towards WCAG 2.1 level AA, the standard US public schools must meet under the ADA Title II web rule. It has not been independently audited.
        • Everything can be used with a keyboard. Describe the map, in the story card, gives a written description of what the 3D map is showing, updated as the timeline moves; screen readers announce it when the timeline is paused or the step changes.
        • The moving streaks and spinning clouds can be stopped in Settings (⚙, top of the story card); this also makes the landslides appear at once instead of fading in. If the device is set to reduce motion, the page starts with them stopped. Pressing Pause stops the timeline.
        • Small screens. On school Chromebooks, iPads and other screens under about 1280 × 780 (but wider than a phone), the card is a little narrower, the time bar slimmer and the map key starts closed; text stays the same size. Two extra buttons appear: full screen (in the time bar, where the browser allows it) and Hide card (under the step buttons), which folds the card to a small strip with the step and its main button so the map can use the whole width. Larger screens keep the usual layout.
        • Listen (🔊, top right of the story card) uses the device's own voice to tell the part of the story the card is showing (the step, a pause or a stage). The spoken words are written to be heard, so they differ a little from the card text. Play the whole story (under the step buttons) runs all seven steps in order, with the narration, the timeline and the camera moves; any button, the timeline or Stop ends it. It is optional and off by default, so it does not clash with screen readers. The page picks a natural-sounding female US English voice if the device has one (for example Microsoft Aria or Jenny in Edge, Google US English in Chrome, Samantha on Apple devices); a different voice can be chosen in Settings. Voices differ by device and browser; Microsoft Edge has the most natural ones.
        • In step 7 the Spatial correlation panel on the map lists the four factors as the story adds them. Each is marked with a tick, an arrow or its number, and screen readers also hear "added", "now" or "still to come"; the card says the same. It folds with its title (on phones it starts folded).
        • Known gaps: the 3D map itself cannot be explored by a screen reader beyond the written description; the river and slope charts have written summaries but no data tables yet.
