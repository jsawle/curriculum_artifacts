      For teachers

      What the 3D view shows
      • Rain so far. The blue shading on the ground is the rain that had fallen since 8 am EDT on Tuesday 24 September, and it grows as the timeline plays. It is made from NOAA's hourly radar estimates (MRMS) at about 390 towns and river gauges, prepared for NOAA's Helene in Southern Appalachia dashboard, spread between those places by inverse-distance weighting. It covers the southern Appalachians only. Click the map to read the value at a spot.
      • Steep ground. Slope worked out from Esri World Elevation on a grid about 1.2 km apart, so it describes the steepness of the landscape (mountain sides, the Blue Ridge escarpment), not of single hillsides.
      • Heavy rain on steep ground. Places where the total rain and the steepness are both above the thresholds chosen in step 6. Students can change the thresholds.
      • River gauges. Each gauge is a column of water whose height is the river's stage, measured every 15 minutes by the USGS. The red ring marks the NWS "major flood" level; the white ring marks the highest the river had been measured before Helene. 1 ft of river is drawn 120 m tall. A grey column means the gauge stopped reporting.
      • Landslides. Points from the USGS preliminary landslide inventory. It does not record when each slide happened, so the page shows them from the morning of 27 September, when most occurred.
      • Weather pattern. The Bermuda High, the upper-level low (a dip in the jet stream) and the stalled front are drawn in approximate positions from the NHC report ("a stationary front … from the central Gulf Coast northward towards the Tennessee Valley and southern Appalachians from 25–27 September"; "a deep-layer cut-off low centered over the Tennessee Valley"; "a subtropical ridge centered over the western Atlantic"). They are not traced from weather maps.
      • Moisture stream. The light-blue streaks sketch the narrow stream of tropical moisture that NOAA's CW3E centre describes as "AR-like poleward transport of enhanced water vapor on the east side of Helene", carried "into the Southern Appalachians on 26 and 27 September", and that Climate.gov describes as squeezed between Helene's anticlockwise spin and the Bermuda High's clockwise spin. The page draws it as a curved band from Helene's east side to the Blue Ridge from 25 September until Helene's own spin takes over; its exact width and path are a sketch. Scientists call this kind of stream an atmospheric river; "conveyor belt" here is a plain-language image, not the meteorological term "warm conveyor belt".
      • Storm and winds. The storm centre follows the NHC best track. The cloud spiral and the moving white streaks are drawings of the storm's anticlockwise spin, not measured winds. The streaks ride about 1.5 km above the ground, so they rise over the mountains, and they turn blue only where the radar measured at least 0.25 in of rain in that hour, so where the "conveyor belt" drops its rain comes from real data.
      • Terrain. Mountains are drawn 2× taller by default. Use the switch at the top to see true height.
      Limitations
      • The rain map is spread between about 390 radar points, so it smooths out small, very wet spots. Radar estimates can differ from rain gauges: the highest gauge total, 30.78 in at Busick, is higher than the radar values nearby.
      • The landslide inventory is preliminary and misses slides hidden by forest or too small to see from satellites. Some points are at the damage site, not where the slide began.
      • Step 6 compares areas, so it shows that landslides and heavy rain on steep ground happened in the same places. It cannot, by itself, prove that one caused the other; the science of why is in step 3 and step 5.
      • River heights from different sources differ slightly: the USGS provisional peak at Asheville is 24.67 ft, the NHC report gives 24.8 ft.
      • Everything comes from live services. If one is down, that layer says so and the rest of the page still works.

      About and versions
      Accessibility
      • The page is being built towards WCAG 2.1 level AA, the standard US public schools must meet under the ADA Title II web rule. It has not been independently audited.
      • Everything can be used with a keyboard. The Map now tab gives a written description of what the 3D map is showing, updated as the timeline moves; screen readers announce it when the timeline is paused or the step changes.
      • Motion on/off (in the time bar) freezes the moving streaks, spinning clouds and the step 7 build-up. If the device is set to reduce motion, the page starts with motion stopped.
      • Listen (beside each step's title) uses the device's own voice to read the step and the map description. It is optional and off by default, so it does not clash with screen readers. The page picks a natural-sounding female US English voice if the device has one (for example Microsoft Aria or Jenny in Edge, Google US English in Chrome, Samantha on Apple devices). Voices differ by device and browser; Microsoft Edge has the most natural ones.
      • Known gaps: the 3D map itself cannot be explored by a screen reader beyond the written description; the river and slope charts have written summaries but no data tables yet.
