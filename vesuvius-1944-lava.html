What happened in March 1944?
  • 18–21 March: lava flows. The flow smashed through San Sebastiano and Massa di Somma, about 5 km from the crater, and reached a total length of 5.6 km.
  • 21–22 March: lava fountains.
  • 22–29 March: explosions. Ash fell to the east and damaged dozens of American B-25 bombers at a nearby airfield.

Lava moved slowly enough that most people escaped. It was the most recent eruption of Vesuvius, which is dormant, not extinct.

How the model works

Lava is heavy and runny, so it tends to follow the steepest path downhill. It doesn't follow that path exactly, because small bumps on the ground push it aside. Scientists model this with a method called DOWNFLOW (Favalli and colleagues, 2005):

  1. Take a real elevation map. Here it's Esri World Elevation, sampled on a 20 m grid.
  2. Randomly raise or lower every point by a small amount (the roughness).
  3. Trace the steepest downhill path from the vent. If the path lands in a hollow, fill the hollow with lava until it spills over, then carry on.
  4. Repeat hundreds of times with different random bumps. Places crossed by many paths are the most likely to be covered.

The colours show the share of simulated flows that crossed each spot. It's a chance, not a forecast of exactly where lava will go.

What this model can't tell you
  • Timing. The replay shows the order the ground was reached, not real hours or days.
  • Thickness and cooling. The model doesn't know how deep the lava is or when it cools and stops. You set the length yourself.
  • Today's ground, not 1944's. The elevation map is modern. It includes the 1944 lava itself, and the crater was reshaped by the 1944 explosions, so an overflow today need not follow the 1944 route.
  • No buildings. The map is bare ground. Walls and streets steer real lava.
  • Only lava. Ash fall, volcanic bombs and pyroclastic flows are different hazards with different models.
Why not the AD 79 eruption?

Pompeii and Herculaneum were not destroyed by lava. At Pompeii, falling pumice and ash piled up to 3 m deep. Then pyroclastic density currents swept through: fast, ground-hugging clouds of hot gas and ash. A lava model can't show that, so this explorer uses 1944, when lava really was the main hazard.
