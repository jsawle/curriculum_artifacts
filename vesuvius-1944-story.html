    For teachers

    This page tells the story of the March 1944 eruption of Mount Vesuvius. First the whole eruption plays on real terrain in about 1½ minutes, with a caption for each of its eight moments. Then students open the moments one by one to see a photograph or eyewitness account, answer a question and replay that part. It was written for grades 6–8 and runs in a web browser, with no sign-in.

    Some ways it could be used

    • Whole class, about 20 minutes: on a projector, watch it through once, then open the moments together for discussion.
    • Pairs or small groups, about 30–40 minutes: students watch, then work through the moments and note their answers.
    • As a starting point for the “what if” explorer: after seeing what happened in 1944, students test what lava might do at Vesuvius or another volcano today.
    • For a history or literacy lesson: the Photos & eyewitnesses button has 12 photographs, 7 accounts and 5 source-analysis tasks.

    Standards it can support

    • NGSS MS-ESS3-2 (natural hazards and forecasting), MS-ESS2-2 (processes that change Earth's surface, including catastrophic events), and, for high school, HS-ESS3-1 (how natural hazards have influenced human activity).
    • Common Core literacy in history/social studies RH.6-8.1, RH.6-8.6, RH.6-8.7 and RH.6-8.9 (primary and secondary sources, point of view, integrating photos, video and maps).

    Notes on the questions

      What is modeled and what is illustrated

      The lava comes from a computer model (the DOWNFLOW method) run on today's elevation data from the two places the 1944 flows started. It follows the most likely downhill path and stops at the heights recorded in 1944. Its timing is set so the northern flow reaches San Sebastiano early on 21 March, as recorded. The ash cloud, lava fountains and ash on the ground are illustrations that follow the directions, heights and dates in the scientific record. They are not modeled.

      If videos or photos don't load

      Most photos load from Flickr and Wikimedia Commons. Some school networks block these. Every item has a link to its source, and the lesson still works without them.
