Why does the air move?

  1. The Sun heats the Earth unevenly. Where the midday Sun is high, its energy is packed onto a small area, so the ground and air get hot. Near the poles the same energy is spread over a much bigger area.
  2. Hot air rises. This leaves low pressure (L) at the ground. The rising air cools, clouds form and it rains. This is the rain belt.
  3. High up, the air spreads out toward the poles. It cools and sinks at about 30°. Sinking air makes high pressure (H) and dry weather.
  4. At the ground, air flows from high pressure to low pressure. This flow is the wind.
  5. The spinning Earth bends the winds (the Coriolis effect): to the right in the northern half of the world, to the left in the southern half. That is why the trade winds blow from the north-east and south-east, not straight from the north and south.

The rain belt follows the overhead Sun north and south through the year, a month or so behind it.

Data sources

  • Wind: Global Average Wind Speeds by Month and Altitude — ERA5 reanalysis, Copernicus Climate Change Service / ECMWF, published by Esri.
  • Average rainfall: Average Global Precipitation — generated using Copernicus Climate Change Service information (ERA5, 1981–2010).
  • Monthly rainfall: NASA GPM IMERG (Huffman et al.), Esri Living Atlas — subscriber content.
  • Land cover: Sentinel-2 10 m Land Use/Land Cover — Esri and Impact Observatory (CC BY 4.0).
  • Cells, surface winds and sunlight: simplified models written for this app (see the versions table), not measured data.
  • Basemap and elevation: Esri.
