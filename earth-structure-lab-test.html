    How do we know what's inside?

    Nobody has drilled through even the crust: the deepest hole ever drilled is about 12 km. Instead, scientists use the waves that earthquakes send through the Earth, recorded by seismometers all round the world.

    Show

    Time after the earthquake: 0 min

    1. P-waves (primary) are longitudinal: the rock is pushed and pulled along the direction the wave travels. They are the fastest, and they travel through solids and liquids.
    2. S-waves (secondary) are transverse: the rock shakes from side to side. They are slower, and they can only travel through solids.
    3. The paths curve. Waves speed up as they go deeper, so they bend back up towards the surface. Where they cross a sudden change, such as the edge of the core, they bend sharply (refraction).
    4. S-wave shadow zone. Beyond about 104° from an earthquake, no S-waves arrive. They are stopped by the outer core, so it must be liquid. In 1914 Beno Gutenberg worked out that the core starts about 2,900 km down.
    5. P-wave shadow zone. Between about 104° and 140°, almost no P-waves arrive, because they bend sharply where they enter the slower, liquid core.
    6. The inner core. In 1936 Inge Lehmann noticed faint P-waves arriving inside the P-wave shadow zone. She showed they had been bent by a boundary deep inside the core: there is an inner core, where the waves travel faster. Scientists later argued that it is solid (1940s–1950s), and in 1971 this was confirmed from the way the whole Earth "rings" after very large earthquakes.

    The paths come from a simplified model of how fast waves travel at each depth, so they land a few degrees from the measured shadow-zone edges, which are drawn from real seismometer records.

    Why do the plates move?

    1. Heat escapes from inside the Earth. It comes from the core, from heat left over from when the Earth formed, and from radioactive elements in the rocks.
    2. The mantle is solid rock, but it flows very slowly, like extremely stiff putty, over millions of years.
    3. Hot rock rises because it is less dense than the cooler rock around it. Narrow plumes of hot rock rise from deep in the mantle, for example under Hawaii, which is in the middle of a plate. Elsewhere, warm rock rises slowly over wide areas to replace the rock that sinks.
    4. Under the ocean ridges, rock is pulled up. As two plates move apart, rock rises to fill the gap. From about 150–200 km down the pressure has dropped enough for a little of it to melt, and the melt makes new plate. This rising rock is shallow: it is pulled up by the plates, not pushed up from the core.
    5. Cold rock sinks. Old ocean plate cools, becomes denser than the mantle below it and sinks at the trenches.
    6. The plates are part of the loop. The sinking edge pulls the rest of the plate after it (slab pull), and new plate slides away from the high ridge (ridge push). Plates with sinking edges move fastest, which is why many scientists think slab pull is the biggest force. The flowing mantle is the rest of the same loop, not a conveyor belt carrying the plates.
    Plume of hot rock rising from deep in the mantle Rock pulled up under a ridge, melting a little Warm rock slowly rising Rock cooling as it moves under the plate Cooler, denser rock sinking Rock moving back along the bottom of the mantle Sinking plate (slab) Plate moving away from a ridge towards a trench (drawn just above the surface so you can see it)

    How fast? Plates move about 2–10 cm a year, roughly as fast as fingernails grow. Here a trip round one loop takes 12 seconds; in the real mantle it takes hundreds of millions of years.

    This is a simplified picture, drawn the same on any cut. The ridges, trenches and plume are spaced evenly to show the idea: they are not the real ones under the places on this cut. Real convection is not neat loops of equal size, and sinking plates can reach the bottom of the mantle.

    Sources: NGSS HS-ESS2-3 and ESS2.B; Columbia University, "Driving forces of plate motions"; Pacific Northwest Seismic Network, "Plate tectonics"; WHOI Oceanus, "The Big MELT" (upwelling under ridges driven by the plates, melting from 150–200 km).

    Sources: National Geographic Education; Earle, Physical Geology; Wikipedia ("Lithosphere", "Asthenosphere"); PREM (Dziewonski & Anderson, 1981). Figures are rounded; scientists' estimates vary.
