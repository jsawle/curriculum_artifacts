For teachers
How the models work

Energy cone (pyroclastic flows and the blast). Picture a cone upside down, with its tip high above the vent. A flow starts at that height and loses a fixed amount of height for every metre it travels, set by the mobility (H/L). It can reach any spot where that falling line stays above the ground. A high ridge stops it; a low one is swept over. Scientists use mobility values from 0.05 (very mobile) to 0.5.

LAHARZ (lahars and the avalanche). From a US Geological Survey method: the bigger the volume, the more of each valley it fills and the more ground it covers. Lahars are runny, so they spread over ten times more ground than a rock avalanche of the same volume. The model follows the river downhill, fills each stretch of valley to the right depth, and stops when it has covered its total area. Close to the summit, everything is treated as a danger zone: ground lower than a cone from the summit that drops 0.23 m for every metre outwards (the value the method's authors used at Mount Rainier), compared square by square as the USGS Laharz_py software does. The map shades only the part of that zone that drains into the valley the flow goes down. Valley-filling starts where the flow leaves the zone.

What these models can't tell you
  • The blast is an approximation. The real blast was a sideways explosion. There's no simple standard method for that, so this uses an energy cone limited to one side of the volcano. The fan faces north and is about 150° wide, as described for 1980. Towards its sides the blast is made less mobile, so it only just gets onto the south side of the volcano. That fall-off is our choice, not a published method. Because the cone is aimed from a single point, some edges near the volcano are still straight.
  • Pyroclastic flows spread too widely. They also use an energy cone. A study of the 1980 flows (McEwen & Malin, 1989) found that this kind of model "generally predicts velocities that are too high, resulting in flow paths that are not sufficiently responsive to the topography". The page therefore sets their mobility by matching the 1980 map: the default flows reach 7.6 km north, as far as the mapped deposits. The shape is the real test, shown by how much of the mapped deposit the model covers. If you move the mobility slider yourself, the page stops setting it.
  • The clock is an estimate. The models show where each hazard could reach, not when. The clock adds speeds reported by the USGS: the blast at least 480 km/h, pyroclastic flows 80–130 km/h (the page uses 105), and lahars up to about 145 km/h on steep upper slopes, slowing to about 5 km/h on flatter ground. In between, the page estimates a lahar's speed from the slope. We didn't find a reliable speed for the avalanche, so it plays in order without a clock.
  • Tidied edges. The map smooths out one-square spikes and fills tiny holes so the flow is easier to read. The distance and area in the results come from the model before tidying.
  • 3D depth. The 3D surface shows how high the model fills each stretch of valley. The real flows were deepest at their peak and left much thinner deposits behind.
  • No photo from before 1980. The satellite photo is modern, so it shows today's crater. With the 1952 ground on, the area around the volcano is drawn as shaded relief from the 1952 heights instead. The colours only show height, not what covered the ground. The nearest map-ready image from before the eruption we found is a 1973 Landsat scene with 60 m pixels, in false colour (plants show red).
  • Only one flow at a time. In 1980 lahars ran down several valleys at once. Run each one separately.
  • The ground. Hazards 1 and 2 use the 1952 mapped surface, the best record of the mountain before 1980. Beyond its edges, and for hazards 3 and 4, the model uses today's ground. Hazards 3 and 4 happened after the avalanche had already reshaped the land, so today's ground is closer to what they flowed over, but it has also changed since 1980.
  • Start points are approximate. The model moves each start to the nearest channel, so check the marker is where you expect. The South Fork, Pine Creek and Muddy River lahars start on the volcano. The North Fork lahar starts at the upstream end of the avalanche deposit in the North Fork valley, about 10 km from the summit. The USGS says it formed from water escaping from inside the deposit, but no source we found gives one exact starting point.
