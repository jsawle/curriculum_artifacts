How the models work

Energy cone (pyroclastic flows and the blast). Picture a cone upside down, with its tip high above the vent. A flow starts at that height and loses a fixed amount of height for every metre it travels, set by the mobility (H/L). It can reach any spot where that falling line stays above the ground. A high ridge stops it; a low one is swept over. Scientists use mobility values from 0.05 (very mobile) to 0.5.

LAHARZ (lahars and the avalanche). From a US Geological Survey method: the bigger the volume, the more of each valley it fills and the more ground it covers. Lahars are runny, so they spread over ten times more ground than a rock avalanche of the same volume. The model follows the river downhill, fills each stretch of valley to the right depth, and stops when it has covered its total area. Close to the summit, everything is treated as a danger zone: an energy cone from the summit with a mobility of 0.23, the value the method's authors used at Mount Rainier. Valley-filling starts where the flow leaves that zone.

What these models can't tell you
  • The blast is an approximation. The real blast was a sideways explosion. There's no simple standard method for that, so this uses an energy cone limited to one side of the volcano.
  • The clock is an estimate. The models show where each hazard could reach, not when. The clock adds speeds reported by the USGS: the blast at least 480 km/h, pyroclastic flows 80–130 km/h (the page uses 105), and lahars up to about 145 km/h on steep upper slopes, slowing to about 5 km/h on flatter ground. In between, the page estimates a lahar's speed from the slope. We didn't find a reliable speed for the avalanche, so it plays in order without a clock.
  • Tidied edges. The map smooths out one-square spikes and fills tiny holes so the flow is easier to read. The distance and area in the results come from the model before tidying.
  • 3D depth. The 3D surface shows how high the model fills each stretch of valley. The real flows were deepest at their peak and left much thinner deposits behind.
  • Only one flow at a time. In 1980 lahars ran down several valleys at once. Run each one separately.
  • The ground. Hazards 1 and 2 use the 1952 mapped surface, the best record of the mountain before 1980. Beyond its edges, and for hazards 3 and 4, the model uses today's ground. Hazards 3 and 4 happened after the avalanche had already reshaped the land, so today's ground is closer to what they flowed over, but it has also changed since 1980.
  • Start points are approximate. The model moves each start to the nearest river, so check the marker is where you expect.
