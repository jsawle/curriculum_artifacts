How the model works

Where the lava goes. Lava is heavy, so it flows downhill and follows valleys. The model takes a real elevation map and traces the steepest way down from the vent, filling any hollow until it spills over. It does this 300 times, each time adding small random bumps to the ground, because real ground has walls, rocks and trees the map can't show. Places many flows cross are the most likely to be covered. This is the DOWNFLOW method used by volcano scientists.

How fast it goes. Lava moves faster when the slope is steeper and the flow is thicker, and slower when the lava is stickier (more viscous). The model uses a simple rule scientists use for thick liquids flowing down a slope (the Jeffreys equation): speed = density × gravity × slope × thickness² ÷ (3 × viscosity). Each kind of lava has its own viscosity, thickness and greatest length.

Who lives in its path. The number of people comes from WorldPop 2020 estimates on a 100 m grid. It counts people who live where at least 20% of the modeled flows went.

What this model can't tell you
  • Exact paths or times. Real lava cools, builds walls and tubes, and changes speed as the eruption grows or weakens. The model gives likely areas and rough times, not a forecast.
  • How much lava erupts. Real flows stop when the supply stops. Here each kind of lava has a fixed greatest length, based on real flows.
  • Other hazards. At many volcanoes, ash, pyroclastic flows and mudflows (lahars) are more dangerous than lava. At Mount St. Helens and Vesuvius, sticky or gassy magma makes explosions the main danger.
  • Today's ground and people. The elevation map and population are from recent years. Buildings, walls and roads also steer real lava.
  • Fast lava rivers. The speed model follows the front of a flow. Near a big vent, lava can run in channels much faster.
  • Craters and lakes. Lava from a summit vent first fills the crater or caldera, and the model doesn't count the time that takes. Lakes are treated as flat ground.
  • Vent positions marked “about” are placed by the app from a direction and height given in the sources.
For teachers

Students pick a volcano, a start point and a kind of lava, then see where lava could go, how soon, and how many people live in its path. It was written for grades 6–8, runs in a web browser and needs no sign-in. Each run takes a few seconds.

Some ways it could be used

  • Compare lava types: keep the volcano and start point, and change only the lava. What changes, and why?
  • Compare volcanoes: each group takes a volcano and reports how far, how fast and who is at risk, then the class compares.
  • Model versus reality: each volcano has a documented real flow to check the model against.
  • After the Vesuvius 1944 story: students test what the same lava might do today, with today's population.

Standards it can support: NGSS MS-ESS3-2 (natural hazards and forecasting) and MS-ESS2-2 (processes that change Earth's surface), with the practice of developing and using models, including their limits.

Notes on the questions

  • Very runny: there may be minutes, not hours, to leave; plans rely on warnings from monitoring and on moving people before lava arrives.
  • Runny: valleys and low ground steer lava; homes in valleys below a vent are most at risk.
  • Medium: thicker lava moves more slowly, so people can leave, but it still buries or pushes over buildings in its path.
  • Sticky: gas trapped in sticky magma can't escape easily, so pressure builds and causes explosions, pyroclastic flows and ash, which this lava-only model doesn't show.

If the map, population or place names don't load, a school network may be blocking Esri or WorldPop services.
